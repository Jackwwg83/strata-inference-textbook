You have surely asked this

You paste in a long piece of code and ask: "Where does the variable on line 3 get changed later?" To answer, the model has to look back through the earlier text, and it has to know where to look. With 10,000 words of earlier text, it cannot look at every word with the same effort.

"Look back with a question in mind, and look harder at the more relevant places": that is attention. This chapter first uses a small example you can work by hand to show how attention is computed. Then it looks at why attention gets more and more expensive as the context grows. Finally it looks at the two tricks Strata's model uses to save effort: letting 24 query heads share 2 sets of notes, and looking at no more than 2051 picked positions each time.

In one line: attention = use the current word's "query" to score every earlier position, turn the scores into weights with softmax, then mix the positions' "values" by those weights. The work of full attention grows with the square of the length. In Strata's model only 12 layers do attention, 24 query heads share 2 sets of keys and values, and each step looks at no more than 2051 picked positions.

8.1 Attention: looking back through the book with a question

Picture yourself looking for material in a library. You have a question in mind: that is the query (written q). Each book has a label on its spine: that is the key (k). What is inside the book is the value (v). You compare your question with every label, read the more relevant books more carefully, and finally blend what you read, in proportion to "how carefully", into an answer.

The attention mechanism: Q, K, V

Inside the model, every earlier position has a vector. The attention layer uses three learned matrices to turn it into three parts: a key k ("what I am") and a value v ("what I can offer"), and for the current position also a query q ("what I am looking for"). The computation has four steps:

  1. Score: take the dot product of the query with each key: sᵢ = q · kᵢ = q₁kᵢ₁ + q₂kᵢ₂ + …. The more two vectors point the same way, the bigger their dot product.
  2. Scale: divide each score by √d, where d is the length (number of dimensions) of the vectors. Dot products naturally get bigger as d grows; without scaling, the softmax in the next step turns extreme and stares almost only at one position.
  3. softmax: wᵢ = esᵢ ÷ Σⱼ esⱼ. The scores become weights that are all positive and add up to 1.
  4. Weighted sum: output = Σ wᵢ · vᵢ.

Work out the smallest example by hand. The earlier text has only two positions, with values [2, 0] and [0, 4]. If both scaled scores are 0, the weights are e⁰ ÷ (e⁰ + e⁰) = ½ and ½, and the output is ½ × [2, 0] + ½ × [0, 4] = [1, 2]. Raise the first score to ln 3 ≈ 1.10, and the weights become 3 ÷ (3 + 1) = ¾ and ¼, so the output becomes [1.5, 1].

Note two things. First, the output is a new blended vector; it does not "copy" a sentence from the earlier text unchanged. Second, k decides "where to look" and v decides "what you see". They have different jobs, and swapping them is generally not equivalent.

Real models run many sets of q, k and v at once, called multi-head attention: each "head" looks for what it cares about, and the results are combined at the end.

How this ties into the chapter: each QSA layer in Strata's model has 24 query heads of 256 dimensions each, so the scaling divides by √256 = 16.

The q, k and v of a QSA layer come from three projection matrices: attn_q, attn_k and attn_v. Code fact[S18] Before a key is stored in the cache, it is "rotated" once according to its position. This is called rotary position embedding (RoPE): the same word at different positions gets slightly different keys, which is how the model knows the order. Values are never rotated (qsa.hpp line 180). After the attention is computed, the result is also multiplied by a sigmoid output gate before going on to the next projection. [S18]

8.2 One more word, one more look: O(n²)

Attention has an iron rule: when generating the t-th word, it may look only at positions 1 to t, never peek at what comes after. This is the causal mask: it covers the "future" positions; see Figure 8-3. While writing the answer, the later words do not exist yet, so the mask makes no difference. But while reading the prompt (prefill, Chapter 1), the whole prompt is computed at once and the later words are already in memory. Every row must explicitly mask the positions to its right; otherwise the model "spoils the ending" for itself.

Computational complexity and O(n²)

To estimate "how slow" an algorithm is, we do not count seconds. We ask: as the input grows, how does the work grow? This is called time complexity, written in big O notation. O(n) means the work is proportional to n: double the input, double the work. O(n²) means it is proportional to the square of n: double the input, four times the work; ten times the input, a hundred times the work.

Big O looks only at the "shape" of the growth and ignores constants and lower-order terms. n(n+1)/2 = n²/2 + n/2; when n is large, n/2 is negligible, and the constant ½ does not change the shape either, so it is O(n²).

Now apply it to attention. Reading a prompt of n words under the causal mask means computing n(n+1)/2 "query–position" pairs. At n = 1,000 that is about 500,000 pairs; at n = 262,144 (the longest context Strata supports) it is about 34.4 billion pairs, and every layer and every head computes them all. O(n²) is barely noticeable on short texts and overwhelms everything on long ones.

How this ties into the chapter: there are only two kinds of ways to cut the cost. Lower the cost of each pair (a smaller constant, the same shape), or cut the number of pairs to compute (a different shape). The selection in Section 8.4 is the second kind; Chapter 9's GDN goes further and turns "looking back at the history" into an O(1) state update.

Further reading: Wikipedia: Time complexity · Wikipedia: Big O notation · Wikipedia: Analysis of algorithms

If you studied CS: attention during prompt reading is a double loop, for i in 1..n: for j in 1..i: score(q[i], k[j]), a triangle of work just like bubble sort. During generation each new word does only one row, which is O(n); but generating m words brings it back to O(n·m) in total.

8.3 GQA: 24 askers sharing 2 sets of notes

For attention to look back at the earlier text, the k and v of every earlier position must be stored. This store is the KV cache (Chapter 17 covers how it is managed in pages). The longer the context, the bigger the KV cache. The first trick to save space is to let several query heads share the same set of keys and values. This is called grouped-query attention (GQA).

Each QSA layer in Strata's model has 24 query heads but only 2 KV heads, with 256 dimensions per head. Code fact[S04] The 24 query heads form two groups: heads 0–11 use KV head 0, and heads 12–23 use KV head 1; see Figure 8-5. The source stresses that the mapping is integer division h ÷ 12, not the remainder h % 2. Both forms produce "correctly shaped" results, but only one of them is this model. [S18]

Let us do the math. Each token, in each QSA layer, stores: 2 KV heads × 256 dimensions × 2 (one K and one V) × 2 bytes (FP16) = 2,048 bytes. The 12 QSA layers add up to 24,576 bytes. A 32K context (32,768 tokens) comes to 805,306,368 bytes, exactly 768 MiB. Teaching estimate

Interestingly, the comment in qsa.hpp writes this amount as "805 MiB". [S18] 805,306,368 ÷ 220 = 768; 805 is the decimal MB you get by dividing the byte count by 106. MB and MiB differ by 4.9%. On small numbers that does not matter, but on a budget of tens of GB it is several GB off. This book uses the itemized result, 768 MiB.

If all 48 layers did attention and each of the 24 heads stored its own K/V, each token would store 48 times as many bytes. At a 262K context, that is the difference between about 288 GiB and about 6 GiB. Teaching estimate At run time, Strata uses 8-bit KV by default above 4K, and from 64K up it also keeps most of the KV in RAM, leaving in VRAM only the part attention reads often. Upstream states that this does not change the values attention reads; it only changes where the KV lives. Upstream report[S03]

8.4 QSA: look at only 2051 picked positions

GQA saves storage. The second trick, which saves computation, is more direct: do not look at everything, look only at what was picked. For each token it generates, Strata's QSA layer first uses a cheap indexer to score every earlier position, picks no more than 2051 of them, and then does real attention only over those positions. Figure 8-6 shows the full flow.

A few key details, all from qsa.hpp:

  • Where 2051 comes from. The selection width is min(n_kv, 2048 + 4 − 1), where n_kv is the number of cached positions. 2048 is the budget for "picking by block", 4 positions per block; the extra 3 are the tail that has not filled a whole block yet. When the context is no more than 2051, the selection is "pick all", and it must be exactly identical to picking all, not an approximation. Code fact[S18]
  • The indexer is cheap. It stores just one 128-dimensional key for every 4 positions (the average of 4 raw keys, normalized and rotated). Code fact[S18] To score, each of the 4 indexer heads computes its own dot product and its own ReLU, and the results are added up, with no softmax and no division by √d (qsa.hpp lines 252–265). Compared with real attention (2 KV heads of 256 dimensions per position, 24 query heads), it touches far less data.
  • Pick the top k, ties by number. The selection returns the highest-scoring positions; on equal scores the smaller number wins, and the result is sorted by number in ascending order. [S18] For algorithms that pick the top k of n numbers, see the "Top-k selection" primer in Chapter 10.
  • Not picked means not there. A position that was not gathered gets a weight of exactly 0 in attention, even if it originally had the highest score. The source does this by setting the mask of those positions to negative infinity, and e−∞ = 0. [S18]

This is not the same thing as the FlashAttention you often hear about. FlashAttention uses tiling, recomputation and carefully arranged VRAM reads and writes to compute the full attention exactly, only faster. [R03] QSA's selection changes the set of positions that take part, so what it computes is already a different function. An engine can use both: pick first, then compute the picked part with an efficient kernel. But the correctness of the two optimizations must be argued separately.

If you studied CS: this is a two-stage "approximate retrieval + exact computation", the same idea as a database that "uses an index to narrow down candidate rows, then goes back to the table for an exact comparison". The difference: a database's index filter never drops a correct result, while this selection may miss a position. So it is part of the computation the model defines, and the engine must implement it as is; it may not swap in some "close enough" way of picking.

8.5 Did the location change, or the set?

An inference engine has two kinds of optimizations that both sound like "read less KV" but are completely different in nature:

  • Only the location changes: the same KV bytes move from VRAM to RAM, or into a different page layout. The values attention reads stay the same, and the results should match bit for bit. Strata's KV streaming is this kind: upstream states that "attention reads exactly the same values; only where the KV lives has changed". Upstream report[S03]
  • The set changes: some history positions are dropped, or a different selection rule is used. This changes the model's input, and its quality must be evaluated on its own.

QSA's selection is a rule of the model itself; and "look at only 2051 per step" does not mean "store only 2051". The next token may pick completely different old positions, so all the KV must be kept. The only thing that can change is which storage level it lives in.

The selection boundary is also delicate. The scores of place 2051 and place 2052 may differ only a hair, and a tiny numerical error can swap a position; what gets swapped is a whole input to attention. That is why Strata stores the indexer keys in FP32 rather than the leaner FP16. The comment did the math: FP16 would put a relative perturbation of about 2−11 on every score, and the selection boundary is exactly where such a perturbation hurts most. The cost of FP32 is 1.5 KiB per token across the 12 QSA layers. [S18] The planned 8-bit indexer keys must first prove themselves with measured data on the "selection flip rate". [S18]

The causal mask must also be considered per phase. qsa.hpp explicitly limits itself to "serving the generation phase only": during generation, every position in the cache naturally comes before the current query, and the only content of the mask is the mapping of the tail. The mask in the prompt-reading phase is per row: query i may look only at whole blocks whose last position is no later than i, plus the positions in the tail. That belongs to a different code path. [S18] Code that passes a single-token test may not pass a causal test on a multi-row prompt read.

8.6 Hands-on: budget a long context

Open this chapter's interactive lab, the "History state budgeter". It uses this chapter's geometry (12 QSA layers, 2 KV heads, 256 dimensions) to compute the KV cache, the window resident in VRAM, and the GDN state that Chapter 9 covers. Suggested tasks:

  1. Change the context from 32K to 128K and confirm the main KV becomes exactly 4 times as large. Then convert the bytes to GiB and check that you have not mixed up GB and GiB.
  2. Change the number of active conversations from 1 to 5, and see which quantities grow 5× and which stay the same.

Then go back to this chapter's two interactive figures. In "One attention step", slowly turn q from "kitten" toward "yarn" and note the angle at which their weights are equal. In "How much effort looking back takes", find the context length at which QSA starts to save work over full attention, and explain it with 2051.

Remember these three

  1. Attention = dot the query with each key → divide by √d → softmax into weights → mix the values by the weights. The output is blended, not copied.
  2. The work of full attention is O(n²): reading a prompt of n words takes n(n+1)/2 pairs. In Strata's model only 12 layers do attention, and 24 query heads share 2 KV heads, which makes the KV cache 48 times smaller.
  3. QSA looks at no more than 2051 picked positions per step (all of them if there are fewer), and positions not picked get a weight of exactly 0. But all the KV must still be kept, because the next step may pick any old position.

Common misconception: "Sparse attention looks at only 2000 positions each time, so the KV cache only needs to store 2000 tokens."

Each step looks at a "working set", not the "full set". The next token has a different query, and the positions it picks may be completely different. So the KV cache must still keep an entry for every history position. You can move the rarely read parts to RAM, but you cannot delete them. Deleting them changes the information the model can see.

Go deeper: frozen parameters, page-table permutation tests, and how to accept a selection implementation

A CUDA graph freezes "parameters that change every token"

To save the overhead of dozens of kernel launches, Strata records each layer as a CUDA graph (Chapter 15 covers this) and replays the graph for every token after that. The trap: when a graph is recorded, the kernels' parameter values are recorded along with it. qsa.hpp audited this problem and found 6 parameters across 5 entry points that change every token: the new position pos, the number of cached positions n_kv, the number of completed blocks, the selection width and so on. [S18]

If you recorded them as they are, the result would be worse than a crash: kv_append would write every token to position 0, scoring and selection would only ever see 1 position, and attention would look at a single cell for the whole conversation. The output would still be finite, plausible-looking numbers, with exactly the symptoms of "the model ignores the context", easily misdiagnosed as a problem with the KV cache, the selection or the weights. [S18]

The fix is a "step state" kept in VRAM: each token writes [pos, n_kv = pos + 1, completed blocks, selection width] exactly once, all kernels read from there, and so the parameter lists stay the same from token to token. The four numbers are computed from pos by a single function, so a caller cannot update just one of them. [S18]

Page-table permutation tests: the layout must not leak into the result

The KV cache is stored in pages. Head h of logical position t lives physically at (table[t / page_size] × n_head_kv + h) × page_size + t % page_size. Within a page, one head's consecutive positions sit next to each other, which is exactly the access pattern attention wants most. To make sure the layout does not affect the result, the tests run the same data with page sizes 1, 4 and 512, shuffle the page table at random, and require the attention output to be exactly identical. [S18] That is the acceptance standard for "only the location changes" optimizations.

The tail positions are always picked

The last few positions that have not filled a whole block have no pooled key of their own. The indexer gives them a score of 109 to guarantee they are picked; this matches llama.cpp's causal input in the generation phase (qsa.hpp lines 258–261). It also explains why the selection width is 2048 + 3: up to 3 tail positions, plus the budget for whole blocks.

How to accept a selection implementation

"Every score error is below 10−4" is not enough to prove that two computations picked the same positions. Acceptance must compare at three levels: whether the picked position numbers match (an exact comparison); whether the gathered K and V data match; and whether the final attention output is within tolerance. You should also build samples with very close scores on purpose, to see when a tiny perturbation moves the top-k boundary, and samples with tied scores, to verify the rule "the smaller number wins". Squeezing the three questions into one "the error is small" hides the real differences.

Say which kind of "lossless" you mean

Changing the location, layout and tiling of data can usually keep results bit for bit identical. Changing the set of data that takes part, or the numerical precision, requires stating its effect on quality separately. For example, upstream's 4-bit KV cache saves half the VRAM, but perplexity on long documents rose by 8%–12%, so the default stays at 8 bits. Upstream report[S03] You cannot simply call every speedup "lossless".
