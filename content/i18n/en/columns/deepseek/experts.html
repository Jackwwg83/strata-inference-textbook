A different engine

Page 1 (overview) kept the books at the level of file sets and speed. This page covers one thing: where the experts live. The UD-Q8_K_XL set holds 288.8 GB of experts in total. An ordinary computer runs the 2-bit set, with about 132 GB of experts. A computer with 12 GB of VRAM cannot hold either set. The engine still runs. It puts into VRAM what fits. It computes the rest on the CPU, in place, and reads those experts from slots in RAM or from the model file. After this page you can answer three questions. By what rule do the experts go into tiers? How does the engine decide how much one machine can hold? And why does the Faster mode dare to use one expert fewer?

In one line: Experts have no “standard placement”, only “this machine’s placement”. At startup the engine sets a VRAM budget from the free memory of each card and fills it from hot to cold. The slot store in RAM (the --ssd-cache cache) sizes itself and bypasses the operating system’s page cache. Experts that fit nowhere are read in place from the SSD. The profile and lookahead make “read early” come before “use”. The Faster mode cuts the experts per token from 6 to 5. It keeps the router’s top 5 and renormalizes their weights. It does not skip any computation. Every number on this page comes with its machine and its file set.

2.1 The expert ledger: 288.8 GB, two placement records, the bill for one token

First, set up the ledger. The model has 40 layers. Each layer has 384 experts. Each expert is about 18.8 MB. The total is 288.8 GB. This is the ledger of the UD-Q8_K_XL set. The experts of the 2-bit set are much smaller, about 8.6 MB each (132 GB ÷ 15,360; the 132 comes from 109 + 23 in placement record two below). Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 3–4, line 15) 40 × 384 = 15,360 experts, and 15,360 × 18.8 MB ≈ 288.8 GB. The two lines of the document agree with each other. Teaching estimate(40 × 384 = 15,360; 15,360 × 18.8 ≈ 288.8, which matches “15,360 of 18.8 MB” in the placement table (line 15); 40 × 384 was already worked out on page 1, and this page only uses it as a check)

The bill for one token. For every token it writes, the model picks 6 experts. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 3–4) 6 × 18.8 = 112.8 MB. This is the upper-limit bill for the UD-Q8_K_XL set when none of the 6 experts is resident (for the 2-bit set, at about 8.6 MB per expert, the same sum is about 52 MB). Experts resident in VRAM are computed on the card. Experts resident in slots are computed in RAM. Only experts that are resident nowhere touch the disk. Teaching estimate(112.8 = 6 × 18.8; which experts are resident follows the rules of section 2.2)

Placement record one: 4× R9700 with 251 GB of RAM, UD-Q8_K_XL. 6,197 experts (116.5 GB) sit in VRAM. 9,163 experts (172.3 GB) go to the CPU side. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 19–23) 116.5 + 172.3 = 288.8 and 6,197 + 9,163 = 15,360. The two sums match the total of the first paragraph of section 2.1 and the expert count in the placement table. Teaching estimate(two additions; the addends come from lines 15 and 19–21 of the document) The idea “spread over several cards, compute in place what is off the cards” is in Chapter 21 (this machine has 4 cards, and the experts off the cards are computed in place on the CPU). This record has one precondition. The machine has 251 GB of RAM in total. The document says that the 172.3 GB on the CPU side needs about as much free RAM to stay cached (original: “about that much free RAM”, lines 19–20). Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 7–8 gives the 251 GB of RAM; lines 19–20 give this requirement)

Placement record two: Strix Halo with 128 GB of unified memory, the 2.00 set. The graphics chip and the CPU share one memory pool. About 109 GB of experts stay resident in the pool. The other 23 GB are read directly from the SSD. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 51–55) The numbers 109 and 23 come from one sentence of the document (about 109 GB resident, “the other 23 GB” read from the SSD). They are the resident-to-direct-read split on this 128 GB machine. They are not “the expert total”. The two records are not comparable. Do not subtract one from the other, and do not subtract either of them from 288.8 GB.

Compared with DeepInfer: A cluster takes another road. It spreads the experts, one by one, over dozens of cards (wide EP, one communication domain of 64 cards, and EPLB copies hot experts onto more cards to carry the load). The transfers use the fast links between cards (see DeepInfer page 2; that column was checked at 774ce244; when you repeat these cluster facts, take that link with them). One computer is the opposite. Each machine keeps its own share and fetches an expert from its own RAM or disk when it needs it. Separate the two premises before you compare the two systems (page 1, section 1.5, has a full comparison table).

2.2 Three tiers: VRAM, slot store, direct SSD reads

First, meet the “slot store” (RAM store). When the experts do not fit in VRAM, the engine does not read them straight from disk. RAM holds a cache of its own. It reads in whole experts and makes room by a replacement policy. The header comment of ds4_store.hpp says it plainly: whole experts are read from the model file unbuffered, and “the OS page cache neither used nor disturbed”. Code fact(ds4_store.hpp lines 1–16) For why the engine builds its own cache instead of using the page cache, see Chapter 6, “Model loading”. The page cache evicts by page (4 KB). The slot store evicts by whole expert. The replacement policy can be LRU. It can also be a heat counter with decay (LFU: every 240 accesses per layer, heat is multiplied by 0.97). The command line has a third option, lfulru (--ssd-policy lru|lfu|lfulru). Code fact(lines 59–70; 0.97 and 240 are on lines 63–64)

How the engine decides how much one machine can hold. It does three sums, once each, at load time. ① Graphics-memory side: the expert budget of each card = (free VRAM of that card − 600 MiB reserve − the reserve for an integrated GPU, which grows with the context) × --vram-experts (default 1.0). The integrated-GPU reserve applies on Windows only: it starts at 4 GiB, and above a 16K context it adds about 0.2 MiB for each further position. Code fact(ds4_model.cu lines 4616–4617; the 600 MiB and the default 1.0 are in ds4_model.hpp lines 29–31) ② Fill order: each layer fills from the highest heat in the profile to the lowest. With no profile, it fills by index: “the hottest experts of the profile first, then by index” (details in section 2.3). ③ A layer with experts on both sides needs one empty VRAM slot for swapping in and out. This holds when the expert cache is on, the layer has at least 2 VRAM slots, and not all its experts fit in VRAM. Code fact(ds4_model.cu lines 4688–4703)

The automatic setting for the RAM tier. When --ssd-cache has no value, the engine sizes it itself. It leaves 12 GB of free RAM to the system and other programs. The result is capped at the total expert size. If it comes out below 4 GB, the store stays off. The cap also has a lower bound of 48 GB: it takes the larger of 48 GB and the total expert size. If the free RAM can hold all the CPU-side experts, the engine copies them all into RAM and builds no slot store. Code fact(ds4_model.cu lines 5361–5380) The document adds one sentence in the same place: this automatic sizing is computed when the model starts, so the tuner does not touch it. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 136–141) With even less RAM, the CPU-side experts are read in place from the SSD: “It runs, slowly.” (it runs, but slowly). Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md line 23)

One more squeeze. The context cache grows in VRAM. Each position takes memory away from the expert cache. So fewer experts stay resident in VRAM. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 99–101) In other words: the context cache and the resident experts compete for the same VRAM. The longer the context, the shorter the list of resident experts.

If you studied CS: This is the memory hierarchy again, inside an inference engine. VRAM is like an on-chip cache. The slot store is like a main-memory cache that manages its own replacement policy. Reading directly from the SSD is like paging. The slot store manages “whole experts”, not 4 KB pages, and it reads through a channel that bypasses the page cache (Chapter 6). Evicting by heat follows the same idea as the cache replacement in Chapter 12 (Chapter 12). “The CPU finishes the experts that the GPU cannot hold” is consistent with the main path (Chapter 13, Chapter 14).

Compared with the Qwen path: The main path’s model is much smaller. There, “what to do with the part that does not fit” is mostly a cache-hit question. In dsv41, the 288.8 GB of experts need tiers on both measured machines, and the slot store deliberately bypasses the page cache (the code fact in the first paragraph of section 2.2). If you carry the main path’s “it fits” intuition over to this page, you will underestimate the gaps in placement and bandwidth.

2.3 The hot-expert profile: three uses of expert-profile.bin

What it is. A count table: how many times each expert of each layer was used. It ships with the model pack. The engine reads <pack>/expert-profile.bin by default. You do not need to pass --expert-profile. Only the value none means there is no profile. Code fact(ds4_main.cpp line 1399) Setup ships two factory profiles (profile-2.00.bin and profile-2.25.bin). It assigns them, by file set, to the 2-bit file sets of ISTA (UD-Q8_K_XL has none). When the model is installed, setup copies them from data/dsv41 into the model pack. If the pack already has a profile, setup does not overwrite it. Code fact(setup.py line 794, lines 8707–8710)

Use one: it decides the resident list in VRAM. For each layer, the resident list takes experts from hot to cold. The hot profile puts “the experts that are often picked” first (the fill order in ① of section 2.2). Use two: it decides the prefetch list. At startup, the slot store reads the hottest experts of the profile before the first token. The rank is “count ÷ expert block size of that layer”, so experts that are hot and small go first. Code fact(ds4_model.cu lines 5525–5543) Use three: it leaves a way out when there is no profile. Without a profile, the first few tokens fill the count table themselves. The first run starts to accumulate from zero. Code fact(ds4_model.cu line 5536)

It writes back. By default, the routing counts of a run are written back to this file. So the placement of the next run is different, and the greedy-decoding output and the KL move with it. --profile-readonly reads without writing. It pins the placement to one profile for comparison tests. Code fact(ds4_model.hpp lines 33–36) The Mac document gives the same warning: its quality numbers were measured in a scoring run with “fixed expert placement” (--profile-readonly). A run that learns placement as it goes moves the last digit. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK_MAC.md lines 53–55)

What it is worth. On the R9700 machine with 251 GB of RAM, a code comment records a cautionary case. The least-hot experts in the VRAM cache were given to the slot store (spare slots, DS4_STORE_SPARE). Without these spare slots, the slot store has to read them from the SSD, and the CPU side waits. On average that is 1.8 misses per token, and they cost 2.9 ms out of 49 ms per token. Upstream report(the machine’s measurement recorded in the comment, not measured again for this page; ds4_model.cu lines 5547–5550) 2.9 ÷ 49 ≈ 6%. The spare slots buy exactly this wait. The hot profile and prefetch are a different matter: they decide which experts to read first at startup. Teaching estimate(the ratio 2.9/49 divides the two measured numbers of the previous lines; when you quote it, take the condition “R9700 machine with 251 GB of RAM” with it)

2.4 Reading while computing: lookahead guesses and the read queue

First, the division of work. Experts that are not on the resident list in VRAM are computed in place by the CPU side. The same sentence of the document (the Mac document, about machines short on RAM) says that on such machines decode is held back first by SSD reads and by the CPU side, and the GPU is not the bottleneck (this is a statement about the 128 GB Mac). Upstream report(DEEPSEEK_MAC.md line 60) Reading is slower than computing. So the engine tries to make the reads run ahead of the compute.

What lookahead is. While layer L is computing, the real input of layer L+1 does not exist yet. The engine applies the gate of layer L+1 to the input of the FFN of layer L. This gives an approximate expert list, so it is only a guess. The guessed list is published, and the CPU side at once sends its own threads to read those experts from the file ahead of time. Code fact(ds4_model.cu lines 1137–1143)

How good the guess is. A code comment records a series of percentages for “matches the real routing”. By rank in the guessed list, rank 1 matches the real routing with a probability of 97%. Then come 91%, 81%, 70%, 57%, and rank 6 has 41%. Upstream report(measurements recorded in the comment, not measured again for this page; the comment says they were taken on a machine called Sun, and does not name the file set; same lines 1141–1142 as above) DS4_AHEAD_EXTRA can guess a few more experts. The part of the list beyond the top 6 of the next layer matches only 29% and 20%. The comment says this is mostly wasted reading on a computer fed by an SSD. Upstream report(same as above) The default is DS4_AHEAD=1: guess the next layer only (set it to 2 to guess the layer after next too; the comment gives no reason for the trade-off between the two settings). The reads that come from guesses queue behind the demand reads (demand first). Code fact(ds4_store.hpp line 505: the original comment says “a guess waits behind every demand read”)

If you studied CS: “Guess plus queue” works like a direct-mapped cache with a prefetcher. A wrong guess does not corrupt data. It only wastes bandwidth (right or wrong, the guess does not change the output; a miss only means a wasted read). “Guesses queue behind demand reads” gives the disk bandwidth first to the bytes you must use, and then to the bytes you may use (Chapter 12, “The expert cache”, Chapter 13, “CPU + GPU”, Chapter 14, “Pinned memory, DMA and async”). One computer bets that “the reads on this machine cannot keep up with the compute”.

2.5 The Faster mode: one expert fewer per token

The model picks 6. Faster uses 5. The model itself routes top-6 for each token. Faster keeps the router’s top 5 and renormalizes their weights. The comment says: “the router’s top N are kept and their weights renormalised (the same selection the full model makes, a shorter list)”. It is not “skip 1/6 of the computation”. It is “a list one entry shorter”. Code fact(ds4_experts_rule.hpp lines 3–6) The top-k selection of Chapter 10, “MoE”, becomes here k going from 6 to 5 (Chapter 10).

Which machines turn it on by default. The document says: computers with 16 GiB of VRAM in total or less, and integrated GPUs such as Strix Halo, go to Faster by default. The engine decides by itself (--experts auto). Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md line 130) The rule in the code is a little wider than the document (with the 0.2.6 defaults chosen, that is DS4_DEFAULTS=026, the multi-card RDNA4 case is switched off). At the call site, a multi-card machine also counts as a default-Faster case when it has 2 or more RDNA4 cards that together hold at least half of the routed experts (multi_rdna4). Code fact(ds4_model.cu lines 2361–2376) The document and the code do not conflict here. The document just does not list every case. This page follows the code. The Mac document adds a note: the Speed choice also works on Mac, but the engine’s own default on Mac is Full (6 experts). The measurements and the scores were made with that default. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK_MAC.md lines 68–69)

Is it worth it. The 5-versus-6 record on the 4× R9700 machine with the 2.25 bpw file set: for an 8K prompt, prompt-processing speed 1339 → 1419 ppts (+6%), decode 78.1 → 81.2 tok/s (+4%). Reference KL 0.10987 against 0.10833. Top-1 agreement 87.59% against 87.53% (the comment does not say which side is which mode. Here the numbers are read in the order “5 against 6”, so the first one is Faster). On the integrated GPU of Strix Halo (Radeon 8065S), the same comparison gives decode +11–13% and prompt +5–7%. Upstream report(all of the above are measurements recorded in source comments, not measured again for this page; ds4_experts_rule.hpp lines 33–38) Before you carry these numbers across machines or file sets, go back to section 1.4 of page 1 and align the measurement conditions (Chapter 27).

How the bill shrinks. With the expert size of UD-Q8_K_XL, 6 experts are about 112.8 MB per token and 5 are about 94 MB. That is 18.8 MB less. The 5-versus-6 measurements above used the 2-bit set, where each expert is about 8.6 MB, so the byte counts differ. −16.7% (1 ÷ 6) is a ratio of counts. It holds for every file set. Teaching estimate(the counts 6 and 5 are in DEEPSEEK41.md lines 3–4 and line 130; 18.8 MB is on line 15; the division gives −16.7%) Where the saved 18.8 MB comes from depends on the placement of section 2.2. If the expert is resident in VRAM, nothing moves. If it is not, what you save is “one slot eviction in the slot store” or “one direct read from the SSD”. In other words: Faster saves transfers. It does not empty the GPU. An expert resident on the card is computed on the card, with 5 or with 6.

The switches for users. On the engine side: --experts 5 / 6 / auto (default auto: the rule decides). When the user has not picked a mode, Strata never passes this flag. The engine decides by its rule: if the rule fires it uses 5, otherwise 6. On the strata launch side: --speed faster|full (--full-quality in scripts). strata launch --plan predicts which mode this machine gets. The prediction knows only two cases: an integrated GPU, and a total of 16 GiB of VRAM or less. It does not include multi-card RDNA4. On 4× R9700 the plan shows Full, while the engine really uses 5. Trust “experts per word” in the engine’s startup log. These switches need DeepSeek engine 0.1.50 or newer (0.1.49.2 does not know --experts; then the whole choice step is skipped and nothing is passed). Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 130–132)

2.6 Packing the experts: two formats and the shape of one expert

Two format sources. The expert side has two kinds of packing: the three official 2-bit sets (IQ1_M 1.75, IQ2_XXS 2.00, IQ2_XS 2.25; the bpw in the file name is a label for the whole file set), and UD-Q8_K_XL, whose experts use MXFP4 and whose other weights use BF16. The manifest in setup.py says it in these words: “the experts in MXFP4 as DeepSeek made them, the rest in BF16”. Upstream report(DEEPSEEK41.md lines 5–6, lines 29–30) Code fact(setup.py lines 803–805) The size and quality table of each set is in section 1.2 of page 1. It is not copied again here (page 1).

What is inside MXFP4. 32 weights form a block. A block is 17 bytes: 1 byte of E8M0 shared exponent (every code value in the block is finally multiplied by a scale of 2^(e−128)), and 16 bytes that hold 32 4-bit E2M1 code values (2 codes per byte; a 16-entry table restores the values). At compute time, the block is dequantized first and then accumulated in F32: “Every product is an F32 sum of exactly dequantised weights times F32 activations”. Code fact(ds4_gguf.cuh lines 9–14) For what quantization is and how code values and scale are restored, see Chapter 4, “Quantization”.

The shape of one expert. One expert = three matrices: gate, up and down, each at its own file offset. The engine reads each matrix unbuffered into a slot, aligned to 4096 bytes. It leaves a gap of 8192 bytes between the matrices (and at both ends of the expert). The span it reads must fall inside the gaps. This is the file-side reason for the “read whole blocks” of section 2.2. Code fact(ds4_store.hpp lines 5–9) Inside one layer, the roles can use different low-bit types: gate and up use one, down uses another. But MXFP4 is never mixed with the low-bit types. Code fact(ds4_gguf.cuh lines 40–42)

If you studied CS: Read sections 2.1–2.6 as one chain. The format (2.6) fixes “how many bytes per expert”. The placement (2.2) divides the bytes among three tiers. The profile and lookahead (2.3–2.4) decide when the bytes are on the way. Faster (2.5) deletes one entry from the list. All four layers decide the same thing: the amount of data to move. For the row-major layout and strides of matrices in a file, see Chapter 3. The method of “counting by bytes” runs the same from page 1 to this page.

Remember these three

  1. 288.8 GB of experts = 40 × 384 experts × about 18.8 MB, and each token uses 6. The two placement records, 4× R9700 and Strix Halo, show that “placement has no standard answer”. Quote every number together with its machine conditions.
  2. The engine computes the placement at startup: VRAM by (free per card − 600 MiB reserve − integrated reserve) × --vram-experts (default 1.0), filled from hot to cold. The RAM slot store sizes itself (free − 12 GB, off below 4 GB) and bypasses the page cache. What still does not fit is read in place from the SSD. The profile decides the list and the prefetch. Lookahead queues the “guess of the next layer” behind the demand reads.
  3. Faster is not skipped computation: it takes the router’s top 5 (Full takes 6) and renormalizes the weights. In the code the default conditions are three cases (≤ 16 GiB, an integrated GPU, and multi-card RDNA4 holding more than half of the experts; the third is off with the 0.2.6 defaults). In the same R9700 test it gives +4% decode and +0.0015 KL. On Mac the default stays Full.

Common misconception: “The Faster mode just cuts 1/6 of the experts, so answer quality must drop clearly.”

Faster changes the “list length”. The routing of the model first gives the names of 6 experts. Faster takes the first 5 and renormalizes the weights. It is the same routing choice with a shorter list (ds4_experts_rule.hpp lines 3–6, code fact). The cost is recorded in the comment. In the 4× R9700 test: KL 0.10987 against 0.10833, top-1 agreement 87.59% against 87.53% (the comment does not say which side is which mode; read in the order “5 against 6”). Decode is 4% faster (lines 33–38 of the same file, upstream report, condition: 2.25 bpw file set). “One expert fewer” ≠ “1/6 less quality”. But how to weigh “moving one expert less per token” against a KL difference of 0.0015 is something you must measure for your own use (Chapter 27).

git -C up grep -n -F "kFasterModeVramMax" -- src/dsv41
git -C up grep -n -F "the same selection the full model makes" -- src/dsv41

git -C up grep -n -F "Faster" -- docs/DEEPSEEK41.md

The first four commands match the code facts of 2.2–2.5. The last one matches the default condition of 2.5 (line 130). Note that the default is auto: when the user has not picked a mode, Strata never passes --experts, and the engine decides 5 or 6 by its rule. --experts 6 is only the explicit way to write the Full mode.

The second trap: ppts is not a synonym of tok/s. The 1339/1419 quoted in 2.5 are prompt-processing speeds, and 78.1/81.2 are decode speeds (as the comment says). If you read the four-digit numbers as decode speed, you reach the opposite conclusion.
